# [Modulation Identification] Gradient-based Adversarial Deep Modulation Classification and Data-Driven Subsampling with Python Code

Abstract Automotive Modulation Classification (AMC) can serve as a core component in intelligent spectrum-efficient wireless communication networks. In recent years, deep learning techniques have been proven to offer superior performance compared to traditional model-based strategies, particularly in distinguishing multiple modulation types. However, such deep learning technologies have also recently been found vulnerable to gradient-based adversarial attacks—attacks that rely on subtle input perturbations and can be easily implemented through interference in wireless scenarios. This paper focuses on an efficient attack method: the Carlini-Wagner (CW) attack. Additionally, this study considers data-driven subsampling scenarios: several recently proposed deep learning-based algorithms for selecting sample subsets can shorten the training time of the final classifier while minimizing accuracy loss. In this scenario, attackers need to make assumptions about the subsampling strategy to calculate the loss gradient. Based on the advanced technology of both sides, this paper evaluates the optimal strategy when the other party's strategy is known differently. Interestingly, in the presence of an informed attacker, this paper finds that defenders can reduce computational costs without losing or only minimally affecting performance.
Introduction Automotive Modulation Classification (AMC) allows receiving devices to identify the modulation type of received signals without requiring manual design or pre-arrangement with the transmitter. This functionality is expected to become one of the core functions of next-generation intelligent spectrum-efficient networks, enabling communication devices to adapt flexibly to various wireless systems without compromising spectral efficiency. To promote the implementation of AMC, gradient-based machine learning (ML) techniques, which have achieved success in computer vision and natural language processing, have recently been applied to AMC research, showing promising application prospects. Compared to traditional modulation classification algorithms based on statistical heuristics, ML methods exhibit better performance in terms of both the number of modulation types and sample size expansion. To validate the viability of this promising technology, this paper focuses on two core issues: robustness against malicious interference and computational efficiency.
1. Gradient-based adversarial machine learning relies on deep neural network stochastic gradient descent optimization for machine learning models, known to be vulnerable to adversarial attacks. Such adversarial samples are constructed by introducing minor perturbations and can cause machine learning models to fail when added to legitimate inputs. These perturbations are obtained through solving an optimization problem: minimizing the perturbation norm while maximizing classification error. In wireless scenarios, smaller perturbation norms mean that attackers only need a small transmit power to introduce subtle additional noise into the signal that is difficult for the receiver to detect, leading to a significant degradation in machine learning model performance. Recent studies have confirmed that machine learning models used for modulation classification are susceptible to such adversarial attacks: Usama et al.'s research has shown that well-known CW attacks can be applied to this scenario; additionally, Kim et al., Hameed et al., and Bair et al. have proposed effective adversarial sample generation algorithms that consider the noise impact of perturbation signals during actual wireless transmission.
2. Data-driven subsampling machine learning models also pose challenges to the computational capabilities of wireless devices, especially during training phases. The long training time of deep neural networks becomes a significant bottleneck in their application in wireless communications—wireless communication requires frequent retraining to adapt dynamically changing environments in real-time. To address this, Ramjee et al. proposed a data-driven subsampling strategy, which uses deep learning models (referred to as subsampling networks) for simulation to downsample the input of machine learning models, thereby effectively reducing the architectural scale of deep neural networks and shortening training time. This algorithm leverages the transferability features of deep learning, meaning that different architectures for the same task share common relevant features; it ranks the contribution of each input sample pair to the classification result using multiple pre-trained modulation classifiers.
3. Research Question
Based on the two core issues mentioned above, this paper investigates the robustness of ML-based AMC models employing different data-driven subsampling strategies against adversarial attacks. Subsequent studies have shown that whether or not the attacker is aware of the subsampling network significantly affects their choice of optimal strategy and the effectiveness of their attacks; similarly, the attacker's assumptions about the subsampling network greatly influence the defensive strategy design. Ultimately, this paper proposes a training strategy that is computationally efficient and minimizes performance loss for systems designed to be completely exposed to attackers.
Methodology Overview: This paper validates the anti-adversarial attack performance of an AMC model based on data-driven subsampling through experiments. The experimental setup and core methods are as follows:
1. The modulation scheme dataset is generated by GNU Radio using the RML2016.10b dataset (a high-reference open-source dataset to ensure reproducibility of results). This dataset contains 10 types of received signal samples with different modulation types, with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) range from -20 dB to 18 dB (step size of 2 dB). Each sample consists of 128 I and Q components, represented as a 2×128-dimensional vector; the dataset comprises 1.2 million vectors, with half being used for training and testing.
2. Subsampling Scheme and Modulation Classifier Model•
Subsampling Scheme: Five subsampling strategies are employed, including four subsampling networks-based schemes (CNN, CLDNN, ResNet, Holistic) and uniform subsampling. Among them, the Holistic subsampling selects the best samples from the subsets selected by CNN, CLDNN, and ResNet subsampling networks; only uses the training dataset to select sample indices.
Classifier Model: Adopting the ResNet architecture consistent with existing research, input dimensions are (1, 2, N) (corresponding to channel count, I/Q components, and sample count), comprising 3 residual stacks and 3 fully connected layers. Each residual stack consists of 1 convolutional layer, 2 residual units, and 1 max pooling layer; each residual unit includes 2 filters with a size of 1×5 in the convolutional layer, and there is a shortcut connection from input to output. The model uses the Adam optimizer and class cross-entropy loss function, batch size is set to 1024, learning rate is 0.001.

4. Threat Model Considers Two Attack Scenarios:
•
White-box Attack: Attacker knows the subsampling scheme used by the target classifier completely;
•
Black-box Attack: Attacker guesses the subsampling scheme used by the target classifier. In both scenarios, the attacker is aware of the target classifier's architecture, hyperparameters, and training dataset. To simulate the attack scenario, an alternative classifier trained using the same ResNet model as the target classifier is used to generate adversarial samples (separated from the performance evaluation model); adversarial samples are constructed using the test dataset through the alternative classifier. In the black-box attack, the performance of each classifier is its average precision over the adversarial samples constructed by all classifiers with the same subsampling rate.
Conclusion: This study investigates the effectiveness of gradient-based adversarial attacks on deep learning modulation classification models that employ data-driven subsampling. The research shows that whether the attacker and defender are aware of each other's subsampling scheme is crucial to their success: the defender needs to understand the attacker's assumptions about the subsampling scheme, and the attacker must be aware of the actual subsampling strategy used by the defender. In the absence of such awareness, this paper establishes the optimal defense strategy (uniform subsampling) and the optimal attack strategy (Holistic subsampling when high subsampling rates, CNN subsampling network when low subsampling rates).
Furthermore, this paper finds that through SNR selective training (using only 0 dB and 18 dB SNR datasets), computational resources can be saved without compromising performance in adversarial scenarios—this training method can shorten training time and, under medium subsampling rates, improve performance in certain scenarios (such as CNN and ResNet subsampling with a 1/2 subsampling rate); however, SNR selective training leads to performance degradation in adversarial scenarios when the subsampling rate is too high (e.g., when the number of samples is 16).
This study lays the foundation for the data-driven subsampling strategies in wireless communication systems using deep learning in adversarial scenarios, with a focus on studying the impact of SNR awareness in such scenarios in the future.
